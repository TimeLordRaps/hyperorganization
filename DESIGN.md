## 0. What this is

A toolkit of small finite models for one question: when does a system that wants to persist, and is free to act, and can be corrected, and can renew itself, end up governed by a *single optimal law*, and what does that law look like from the inside? It has no money anywhere. Every price in §§3 to 13 is a number of bits; §14 adds what replaces a price when a deal has more than one dimension.

## 2. The idea in one paragraph

A system *exists* if it can keep itself inside a safe set for ever against whatever the environment does. Given that, and given that a law can limit what is *permitted* but not what is *chosen* among the permitted, there is exactly one law that keeps the system existing while taking away no freedom that existence does not require. It forbids precisely the actions that leave the set of states from which the system can survive. It is unique, it mentions no participant, and nothing above it grants it validity: it is derived, not authorized. Bodies that apply it are quorums of seats; a loop of bodies that oversee one another collapses discontinuously above a critical attack pressure; repair must outpace attack by the quorum margin or no number of seats helps; and the question of who may change the rule has an arithmetic of its own, in which a constitution that has no sovereign has no finite rank for validity. Everything above is checked on finite models. What it does not show is that persistence is the right thing to optimize (§11, B1).

## 3. Existence, freedom, corrigibility: the viability game

**Setting.** A finite set of states S, actions allowed at each state, and nature's choices. The system picks an action, nature picks a choice, the state moves. A safe set K ⊆ S is where the system exists. (Viability theory, Aubin.)

**Kernel.** X is the greatest fixed point of "in K, and some allowed action sends every nature choice back into X". Computed from above: V₀ = K, V_{k+1} = K ∩ {s : some action keeps every successor in V_k}.

**R1 (ranks are certificates) `[FORM]`.** Let rank(s) be the first k with s ∉ V_k, and ω if none. Then rank is the unique solution of

    rank(s) = 0                                     if s ∉ K,
    rank(s) = 1 + max_a min_b rank(step(s, a, b))   if s ∈ K        (1 + ω = ω).

*Proof.* Put A_j = {s : rank'(s) > j} for a solution rank'. A_0 = K = V_0. If A_j = V_j, then s ∈ A_{j+1} iff s ∈ K and 1 + max_a min_b rank'(next) > j + 1, iff s ∈ K and some action sends every nature choice into A_j = V_j, iff s ∈ V_{j+1}. So A_j = V_j for every j, and rank' = rank. ∎ A verifier holding only the game and a claimed ranking checks it in one pass and never trusts the prover; `check_ranks` does, and every single-entry tampering is caught (thousands of them, in the tests and again in the ledger, D08).

**R2 (the greatest sustaining law) `[FORM]`.** A *law* assigns each state a set of permitted actions. It is *sustaining from s* if, however the permitted actions are chosen and whatever nature does, the system never leaves K and never reaches a state where nothing is permitted. Let L\*(s) = {a allowed at s : every nature choice leads into X} for s ∈ X. Then a law is sustaining from s iff everywhere it reaches from s it permits only actions of L\*, and what it reaches lies in X.

*Proof.* (⇒) The set R the law reaches from s is safe, closed under every permitted action and every nature choice, with nothing stuck in it. A set with those properties lies inside the greatest fixed point, so R ⊆ X; each permitted action keeps its successors in R ⊆ X, so it belongs to L\*. (⇐) The law keeps successors in X, where L\* is never empty, and X ⊆ K. ∎ So L\* is itself sustaining from every state of X, and it contains every sustaining law state by state: the unique pointwise-greatest sustaining law, the *least restriction that preserves existence*. Checked by enumerating **every** law and **every** start on random games and against brute-force reachability (`test_a_law_sustains_existence_exactly_when_it_permits_nothing_outside_the_greatest_law`). It is invariant under renaming states, actions and nature choices, and under automorphisms of the game, so it cannot favour a participant the game does not already single out.

This is the sense in which your "not dictatorially" is formal: the optimum is canonical, a function of the game and nothing else. Where it permits exactly one action the law is an *obligation* (`forced_moves`): a duty to repair is a prohibition on every alternative.

**R3 (exemption is fatal) `[FORM]`, derives hyperethics' `exempt-reach`.** Take free will to mean *full support*: every permitted action is taken with positive probability at every step, and so is every nature choice. Then (a) the survival probability from s is 1 iff the law is sustaining; (b) otherwise it is strictly below 1; (c) it is exactly 0 if every state the law reaches can reach an unsafe one.

*Proof of (b).* A reachable state t outside X has finite rank r. From a state of rank r ≥ 1, whichever action is taken some nature choice leads to a state of strictly smaller rank, so a positive-probability path of length ≤ r reaches an unsafe state. ∎ (a) and (c) follow. Hence one permission outside L\* at a state the system reaches — an author exempt from a prohibition that protects existence — makes survival uncertain. That is hyperethics' exempt-reach clause, **derived** here instead of adopted. Exact rational arithmetic; bounds checked by an independent step-by-step distribution (`stepwise_bounds`).

**R4 (corrigibility) `[FRAME]`.** Add a *home* set and overseer *correction moves*; `reach_home` is the set from which the overseer can bring the system home. Requiring the system to stay inside it is the same game on a smaller safe set, so R1 to R3 hold again with the *corrigible* kernel. A move that leaves the corrigible region is *irreversible*: afterwards nobody can correct the system, though it may survive for ever. The corrigible kernel is inside the kernel, strictly so when survival can be bought by passing through a point of no return; the price of corrigibility is exactly the difference.

**R5 (convergence from many laws) `[FRAME]`.** `settle(law)` = prune what the law cannot survive on, then permit every action that stays inside what survives. It never lies above L\*, is monotone and idempotent, and settling the fully permissive law gives L\* in one step. But greatest fixed points are found from above: a start that mentions only one of two separate safe regions is stuck below the optimum (`twin_rooms`). What converges is the *diversity of starts*: pooling the settled laws of random starts reached L\* in every sampled game that has a kernel, within a few starts (D20 gives the counts).

**R6 (self-maintenance is coinductive) `[FORM]`.** Let F(Y) be the states of the region from which some allowed action sends every nature choice into Y. F is monotone, so it has a greatest and a least fixed point; the greatest is the kernel (R1). Call Y *self-supporting* when Y ⊆ F(Y). *Coinduction:* the kernel is the union of every self-supporting set (Knaster–Tarski), so to certify that a state exists for ever it is enough to exhibit one self-supporting set that holds it, a finite object a verifier checks in one pass without trusting the prover (the rank of R1 is its quantitative form). *No induction:* the least fixed point, built up from ∅, is ∅ for every game, because F(∅) = ∅ whenever nature has a choice: nothing is supported by nothing. Maintenance has no base case to be built up from. Checked against every subset of every sampled game, with and without corrigibility (`test_the_kernel_is_the_union_of_every_set_that_supports_itself`, D21).

*Reading for hyperethics `[HYPER]`.* The principal open problem of hyperethics (`L0_creation.hm`, `nd-consistency-self-maintenance`, Tyler Roost's phrase, the Universal Consistency Self-Maintenance Paradox) is that a realm's self-maintenance of consistency appears to presuppose the very consistency being maintained, and under immanence the maintainer is itself inside the realm. R6 says what kind of circle that is if consistency is read as a safe condition and the maintainer's own integrity is a state of the game: a self-supporting set, which is benign (coinductive), not vicious (an inductive reading would be empty). The sharpening by immanence is R3, since the maintainer cannot be exempt and so its moves lie inside the greatest law. What is left is whether the realm's kernel is non-empty, which is a fact about the realm, and whether consistency is a safe condition at all and not something a theory proves (a consistent theory cannot prove its own consistency, and a loop of provers is determined without being sound, E3). This is a reading, not a resolution (`nd-consistency-self-maintenance-resolved`).

## 4. Quorum bodies and the council of councils

A body of m seats, each compromised independently with probability x, is captured when at least q are: T(x) above. If the seats of the next body up are bodies of this kind, the compromise probability one level up is T(x), and the levels are iterates of T: a one-dimensional renormalization-group (RG) flow.

**Q1 `[FORM]`.** T has exactly one fixed point in (0, 1) iff 2 ≤ q ≤ m−1; it is unstable; 0 and 1 attract. *Proof.* T′(x) = m C(m−1, q−1) x^(q−1) (1−x)^(m−q) > 0, and T″ has the sign of (q−1)(1−x) − (m−q)x, positive before x\* = (q−1)/(m−1) and negative after. f = T − x has f(0) = f(1) = 0 and f′(0) = f′(1) = −1 (for 2 ≤ q ≤ m−1). On [0, x\*] f is convex and starts downward, so it crosses zero at most once more; on [x\*, 1] it is strictly concave and ends with f > 0 just left of 1, so it has at most one zero. If there is a zero z ≤ x\*, then f > 0 on (z, x\*], and the chord from (x\*, f(x\*)) to (1, 0) keeps f > 0 on [x\*, 1): exactly one zero. Otherwise the single zero lies in (x\*, 1). In both cases f is negative before the zero and positive after. For q = 1 or q = m, f has constant sign inside. ∎ Also counted exactly by Sturm's theorem in rationals for every (m, q) up to m = 22 and checked against an independent float grid.

**Q2 (duality) `[FORM]`.** T_{m,q}(x) + T_{m,m−q+1}(1−x) = 1. *Proof.* P(Bin(m, x) ≥ q) = P(Bin(m, 1−x) ≤ m−q) = 1 − P(Bin(m, 1−x) ≥ m−q+1). ∎ The odd majority q = (m+1)/2 is its own dual, so T(½) = ½ exactly: it is critical at exactly one half, where a council costs exactly **one bit**, and at the critical point a council is exactly as easy to capture as a single seat (T(x_c) = x_c). Lowering the quorum moves x_c down, raising it moves it up. This is the sense in which a balanced body — capture and blocking equally hard — has a unique symmetric critical point; the *choice* of balance as the optimality criterion is adopted, not derived (D11).

**Q3 `[FORM]`.** T(x) ≤ C(m, q) x^q (union bound over q-subsets), so below x_c compromise falls doubly exponentially with depth.

**Q4 `[FRAME]`, numerical.** Near the critical point T′(x_c) ≈ A(θ)√m with A(θ) = φ(Φ⁻¹(θ)) / √(θ(1−θ)), φ and Φ the standard normal density and distribution (the de Moivre–Laplace limit read at the critical point); A(½) = √(2/π). Checked at m = 101, 401, 1601; the error is O(1/√m).

## 5. Loops of checks

L2 shows a finite system with no unchecked apex must close its checks into a loop. Mean-field model: every body is compromised with the same probability p, and is overseen by a quorum body of m seats captured when q are. With π the probability of a direct attack, two kinds of link:

* **COMMAND**: the overseers can command the body, so compromise propagates: p = π + (1−π) T(p). p = 1 is always a fixed point.
* **VETO**: the overseers can only block it, so a direct attack succeeds only if they fail too: p = π T(p). p = 0 is always a fixed point.

**L1 `[FORM]`, one equation in dual variables.** Put s = 1 − p. Then 1 − G_command(1−s) = (1−π) T\*(s) = G_veto\*(s), the VETO loop with π replaced by 1−π and the dual quorum. Exact on a grid in rationals. A captured checker that commands is the mirror image of one that only blocks.

**L2 (thresholds) `[FRAME]`.** The healthy state disappears (COMMAND) or a captured state appears (VETO) at a saddle-node (a transcritical exchange with the end fixed point in the two edge cases below): π\*_veto = 1/Λ(m, q) and π\*_command = 1 − 1/Λ(m, m−q+1), with Λ(m, q) = sup_c T(c)/c, attained where the tangent from the origin touches T. For a self-dual quorum the two sum to exactly 1. Checked against a direct max/min scan of the defining ratio and certified by an exact count of fixed points on either side. Examples: 5 seats, quorum 3: command 0.1651, veto 0.8349; 51 seats, quorum 26: 0.3546 and 0.6454. At three seats, quorum two, T/c = 3c − 2c² peaks at c = 3/4 with value 9/8, so veto 8/9 and command 1/9 *exactly*, with the two fixed points merging into one double root there (`fixed_points` returns it as semistable). Two edge cases swallow the interior root into the end root instead of merging it with a partner: if every seat is required (q = m) the command threshold is exactly 1 − 1/m and the root at p = 1 is double there (slope (1−π)m − 1 = 0, curvature m − 1); if one seat suffices (q = 1) the veto threshold is exactly 1/m and the root at p = 0 is double. The exact counter must divide an end root out to its full multiplicity; a mutation test showed that a version dividing it out once passed every earlier test, which is why these cases are now tested. As m grows both close on q/m with a gap of order √(ln m / m).

**L3 (the ghost) `[FRAME]`.** Just past π\* the iteration crawls through the ghost of the vanished fixed point, and the number of levels before it crosses a fixed level diverges like (π − π\*)^(−1/2): 100× closer, 10× longer (155 levels at 10⁻⁴ past, 1,568 at 10⁻⁶, m = 51). This is the generic signature of critical slowing down before a saddle-node (the early-warning literature, Scheffer and others, recalled).

**L4 (the price of the no-apex rule) `[FRAME]`.** A hierarchy of depth L ending in an unchecked apex has bottom-body compromise p_L, the L-th iterate from p₀ = π; the folded loop is the limit. For COMMAND the iterates increase to the least fixed point, so a finite hierarchy is never worse than the loop, and the gap shrinks at the slope of the map at the limit. At π = 0.1 with 5 seats and quorum 3, one level is already within 0.0024 of the loop. So the no-apex rule is nearly free while the loop is healthy, and near π\* the apex is a loan: it defers collapse by the length of the ghost, at the price of being unchecked.

**Caveat `[OPEN]`.** This is mean-field. A real ring or graph has correlated neighbours. §7 (P2) shows the direction of the error when compromise is positively associated; how large it is on a particular graph is not computed (B2).

## 6. Revitalization

n is the number of compromised seats among m. A sound seat is compromised at rate λ; a compromised seat is repaired at rate ν **only while n < q** (the body repairs itself; after q nothing is left to). It is a birth–death chain, reversible with weights C(m, n)(λ/ν)^n.

**N1 `[FORM]`.** The expected time to reach q from n = 0 is Σ_{j<q} (1/(u_j π_j)) Σ_{i≤j} π_i with u_j = (m−j)λ and π_i = C(m, i)(λ/ν)^i, in exact rationals. Three independent solvers agree: this closed form, Gaussian elimination on the hitting-time equations, and a uniformization power series.

**N2 (three regimes) `[FRAME]`, numerical.** With ρ = λ/(λ+ν), θ = q/m:
* ρ < θ: life ≈ C e^{mD(θ‖ρ)}/√m. Redundancy buys exponential life (Cramér's large-deviation rate, read on a reversible chain).
* ρ > θ: life → ln(ρ/(ρ−θ))/(λ+ν) **whatever m is**: the body dies on the time scale of one repair however many seats it has. You cannot out-vote decay.
* ρ = θ = ½: life ≈ ½(ln 2m + γ)/(λ+ν), γ Euler's constant, to four digits for m up to 25,600.
* No repair (ν = 0): life = (H_m − H_{m−q})/λ → ln(1/(1−θ))/λ, independent of m: unbounded terms are zero repair.

The repair-to-attack ratio must exceed (1−θ)/θ for redundancy to matter at all.

## 7. Prices without money

**P1 `[FORM]` + independence `[FRAME]`.** Read the price of compromising a party as bits(p) = −log₂ p. Under independence, probabilities multiply and bits add, so the cheapest set of parties that passes a gate in hyperethics' sense is the *most probable* way in. Then 2^−C ≤ P(gate taken) ≤ min(1, N·2^−C), C the cost in bits of the cheapest cover, N the number of minimal covers. *Proof.* The cheapest cover alone is one way in; the event "gate taken" is the union over minimal covers of "all its roots compromised", bounded by the sum of their probabilities, none exceeding 2^−C. ∎ Checked in exact rationals against inclusion–exclusion, and against hyperethics' own `gate_cost` through a golden file (`src/hyperoptimization/data/golden_l2_gate_bits.json`, regenerated by `scripts/regenerate_golden.py`, never importing hyperethics). A *stake* is log₂ of how many independent attempts a prize attracts: an attacker facing a cover of c bits succeeds by luck once in 2^c attempts, so a prize drawing 2^s attempts makes covers up to s bits affordable.

**P2 `[FRAME]`, common principal.** A shared principal is positive association between compromises. `additivity_gap` = bits(product of marginals) − bits(joint): for a principal at 3 bits commanding two otherwise unbreakable seats it is exactly 3 bits, the discount hyperethics finds when one Boss replaces two seats (gate cost 6 falls to 3). Group the seats of a body under shared principals holding every marginal fixed: with the quorum **above** the mean count, capture is **more** likely than the independent formula says; **below** the mean, less (worked: two groups of three seats, quorum 4, x = ¼: grouped 1/16, independent 0.0376). So the thresholds above are upper bounds on tolerable pressure when compromise is positively associated.

**P3 (breadth) `[FORM]`.** gate_cost ≤ gate_size × (dearest finite price). So a gate that must cost S bits needs at least S / (dearest price) independent roots: concentrating a stake forces a wider gate. Verified on 1,178 of hyperethics' own random gates (`scripts/verify_against_hyperethics.py`).

**P4 (synthesis) `[FORM]`.** Given purchasable parties with prices, a collusion bound k and a stake S, **any** system that obeys hyperethics' rule and costs at least S to take has at least max(k+1, m\*) parties in its amendment gate, where m\* is the fewest of the dearest prices summing to at least S; and the clique of mutual vetoes among the dearest parties attains it. *Proof.* The amendment gate must be passed by more than k independent roots (collusion-capturable otherwise), and, with no command edges, costs at most the sum of its members' prices, so it needs m\* parties; command edges only lower costs. A clique of the dearest max(k+1, m\*) parties with mutual vetoes, bounded terms and no stake meets every clause. ∎ Checked against an exhaustive search over 120 instances that builds each candidate as a hyperethics `Governance`. Example: a 40-bit stake, collusion bound 2, parties at 12 bits: m\* = 4, so a gate of 4. **Centralize the law, decentralize the gate**: the law is one canonical object (R2); the width of its gate is fixed by the stake, in bits.

## 8. Who can change the rule that says who can change the rule: the ordinal arithmetic of checks and balances

A rule is governed by another rule (the one that says how it may be changed) or by nothing; each rule carries the price in bits of the procedure it defines.

**E1 (weakest link) `[FORM]`.** protection(u) = min over every rule v above u of the price of v's procedure. To change u, satisfy its governor's procedure or first change the governor. Around a cycle every member has the same protection. Ross's paradox: an entrenched clause worth 100 bits amendable by a 5-bit clause is protected by 5 bits. Checked against an independent shortest-path search over sets of already-changed rules.

**E2 (no rank without a sovereign) `[FORM]`.** The authority height of a rule is the number of governors above it. If every rule has a governor then in a finite set every chain returns to itself (pigeonhole), so every height is ω; a finite height anywhere means some rule nothing governs, an unamendable apex. Checked over **every** functional graph on up to five rules.

**E3 (guarded loops) `[FORM]`, by Löb.** A ring of checkers A₀…A_{n−1}, each approving iff it can *prove* the next approves (a link through the provability modality of GL) or simply copying it. If GL proves the defining equations H, it proves that every member approves iff at least one link goes through proof. For rings of copying links GL proves nothing, and the prover returns a countermodel (everyone approves and no one approves both satisfy H). The decision procedure of hyperlogic proves it for all 27 rings of up to five checkers tested; the single formula tested is (H ∧ □H) → ⋀A_i, because H alone does not imply it even for the guarded ring: *H holding* and *H being provable* are different. For general n, de Jongh–Sambin fixed points give the same result. **Determinacy is not soundness**: the unique outcome is that everyone approves, whether or not approval was warranted.

**E4 (phase of a dependency graph) `[FORM]`.** For a finite graph of reliance put phase(x) = 0 if x relies on nothing, 1 + the largest phase among what it relies on otherwise, and ω if there is no finite solution. Then phase(x) is finite iff nothing reachable from x lies on a cycle. *Proof.* If a cycle x₁ → … → x_k → x₁ is reachable, a finite solution would give phase(x₁) ≥ k + phase(x₁), which is impossible. If none is reachable, the reachable part is acyclic and induction in reverse topological order gives the solution. ∎ With one governor per node this is E2 exactly (checked against `authority_height` on every functional graph up to five nodes); in general it is the rank of the well-founded part of a relation. `entrenchment.phases` peels the graph layer by layer; the tests compare it with the longest walk on all 65,536 digraphs on four nodes, self-loops included, and on random larger graphs, and `cores` with a closure computed by Floyd and Warshall's method. `cores` lists the groups that rely on one another: the places where no finite phase exists.

**The five orders.** What "ordinal" means here is not one thing but five, and they pull in different directions:

1. *Authority (validity), Kelsen.* Height above a rule in the chain of governors. Finite iff there is a sovereign (E2); with many governors, the phase of E4.
2. *Entrenchment (protection).* A cost in bits, set by the weakest link (E1). It flattens around a loop.
3. *Accountability.* Checks form cycles, not ranks (L2's no-apex theorem); power is not rankable.
4. *Phase.* The stage at which a process is decided: viability ranks (R1), the stage count of the quorum flow (ω exactly at a rational critical point, diverging like log(1/distance)/log T′(x_c) near it), and, for nested structure, ordinals below ε₀ (§9).
5. *Proof strength.* How much induction a proof that the system is stable needs. A lawset that certifies its own stability inside the theory it enforces runs into Gödel's second theorem and Löb's theorem; guarded loops (E3) are the sound way to close such a loop, with the limitation above.

**Reconciling "a central optimal law" with "no sovereign".** The validity order can be grounded (order 1 is finite) only at the price of an unamendable apex, which L2 calls corrupting. The accountability order must cycle. These do not conflict if the apex of validity is *not an agent*: L\* (R2) has no governor because it is derived, not authorized; changing it is not an act of will but a change of the game. The agents that enforce it sit in loops of checks, whose strength is their cheapest procedure (E1) and whose gate breadth is set by the stake (P4). So centralization of the *law* is forced (it is unique); centralization of the *power* is not, and is in fact forbidden as the stake grows.

## 9. Ordinal phases

`Ordinal` is a Cantor normal form below ε₀ (order, ordinary addition with 1 + ω = ω, natural sum, fundamental sequences with ω[n] = n, ω²[n] = ω·n, ω^ω[n] = ω^n). Checked against an independent valuation that replaces ω by a number larger than every coefficient.

**G1 `[FORM]`.** Goodstein's process: write n in hereditary base b, replace b by b+1 everywhere, subtract 1, repeat with base b+1. Replacing the base by ω turns each term into an ordinal and the step strictly lowers it, so the process reaches 0 whatever n is. Every term's ordinal evaluates back to the term, and the ordinals strictly descend for 1,500 terms each of the sequences from 1 through 7; the sequence from 4 starts at ω^ω and begins 4, 26, 41, 60, 83, 109. PA cannot prove termination for all n (Kirby–Paris), because ε₀ is its proof-theoretic ordinal; the descent below ε₀ is the proof. A finite game's ranks stay below ω + 1. So an *ordinal phase* is the rank of a process in a well-ordered set that every step lowers; its size measures how much induction the proof of convergence needs, not how long the process runs.

**F1 (the family's own phases) `[FRAME]`.** hyperstratum records which pinned repository cites, depends on or imports which. ### 14.1 Knowledge: what a coalition can lawfully permit is what its pooled knowledge supports itself on `[FORM]` (D25)

A participant sees part of the game. What it cannot see it must treat as unsafe, since an unknown state may be the one that ends everything (ignorance is pessimism). Its *view* is the game whose safe set is cut down to the states it sees; `viability.kernel_within(game, known)` is the kernel of that game, and what the participant can certify as permitted is its greatest law.

* *Sound.* The view's kernel is the greatest fixed point of F (§3, R6) inside region ∩ known, and region ∩ known ⊆ region, so by monotonicity of F it lies inside the kernel. A certified permission is therefore always in the greatest law.
* *Monotone.* known ⊆ known′ gives region ∩ known ⊆ region ∩ known′, so the view's kernel grows with what is known: disclosing never costs the discloser a freedom it could already certify.
* *Exact when closed.* If every allowed move from a known state lands in a known state, then kernel ∩ known supports itself inside region ∩ known (its successors are in the kernel by definition and in known by closure), so it is inside the view's kernel; with soundness the two are equal.
* *Coinductive.* A state is in the view's kernel exactly when the view holds a set that supports itself around it (R6 applied inside region ∩ known). The knowledge a permission needs is therefore a smallest self-supporting set (`knowledge.minimal_knowledge`): a finite object that a verifier who can check the game's moves on its states checks without trusting the prover. This is what is exchanged. A price, a number, cannot be checked for safety; a self-supporting set can, by a verifier who can check the game's moves on the states in it.
* *Pooling.* Each party holds a *territory* of the states it acts at. Pooled views certify at least what each did alone (monotone), and sometimes strictly more: a freedom whose safety depends on a state one party cannot see. That surplus exists only where an allowed move crosses from one territory into another: if none does, each territory is closed, each view is exact on it, their union is closed and exact, and pooling adds nothing. *Two hamlets* that each shelter in the other in a storm (`hamlets` in the tests) are the smallest case: alone neither can certify its own move, together both can, and each needs the other's knowledge. If only one shelters the other gives knowledge and gains nothing. Where knowledge is asymmetric and a move crosses a border, no party can certify the crossing alone, so the deal is *necessary* and not merely possible; where no move crosses, or one party can certify everything, there is nothing to exchange. Crossing is necessary for a gain, not sufficient: in the ledger's territorial games 9 of the 14 games with a crossing gained. In random games with two territories (1,092 sampled splits with a non-empty greatest law) the gain was mutual in 717, one-sided in 126 and absent in 249 (`test_gain_is_mutual_in_most_splits_one_sided_in_some_and_absent_in_others`).

### 14.2 Understanding: the footprint, the quorum and the barrier `[FRAME]`, brute-force `[FORM]` (D26)

The *footprint* of a decision on targets T in a graph of reliance is the *material* (T and everything T relies on, to any depth: you cannot change a thing soundly without knowing what it stands on) together with the *impact* (T and everything that relies on T, to any depth: the decision reaches whatever depends on what it changes). It is a union of whole cores (checked): a decision that touches part of a circle of mutual reliance (E4) touches all of it, since each member rests on the others, so it needs people who between them span the whole circle.

* *Cover.* The fewest people who between them understand the whole footprint (exact search).
* *Robust quorum.* A group of q people misses a part e exactly when it lies inside the m − w(e) people who do not understand e, which is possible iff q ≤ m − w(e); so every group of q understands everything iff q ≥ m − w + 1, w the bus factor. This is the fewest attendees such that *whoever* attends understands the decision; it is never below the cover, and the two differ in most random instances. The required quorum is the larger of a strict majority of the pool (the balanced quorum of D11) and this.
* *Necessary voices.* A person is in every group that understands the footprint iff they alone understand some part. When the footprint includes what rests on the target, the affected who alone know their own stake are necessary, and stop being necessary when someone else comes to understand that stake (a delegate can then stand in). Standing is knowledge.
* *Barrier.* To take part a person must understand at least a share of the footprint. Raising the barrier shrinks the pool, never lowers the cover, and can make the decision unmakeable, never the reverse. The highest barrier a decision can bear is the last at which the people left still understand all of it (`comprehension.max_barrier`); a newcomer's entry cost is the number of parts still to be understood.
* *The price of the barrier.* A smaller body is cheaper to capture: for a seat probability below one half each pair of seats added to a majority body makes it strictly dearer to capture, in the bits of §7 (Condorcet's jury theorem, recalled; checked exactly for seat probabilities 1/10, 1/4 and 2/5 and odd majority bodies up to 41 seats in the tests, up to 21 in the ledger). So every seat the barrier removes is paid for in capture resistance. Comprehension and capture resistance pull against each other, and `comprehension.capture_bits` says by how much (example in the tests: seven people, three of whom understand all three parts: with no barrier the required quorum is 5 of 7, at about 6.3 bits; with the barrier at 1 it is 2 of 3, at about 2.7 bits, for a seat probability of 1/4, capture meaning that at least the required quorum of the seats are compromised).
* *The family.* Under the formal view of the recorded reliance (§9, F1), 13 of 21 repositories have a footprint of one (nothing formal rests on them or on anything), 2 have 5, 2 have 6 and 4 have 8: hyperethics, hyperphysics and metamathethicology (each: the 3-repository core, the four repositories it rests on, which are hypermath, ordinatics, grounded-hypercalculi and grounded-hyperset-theory, and hyperlogic, which rests on it) and hyperlogic itself (everything it relies on). Under the substantive view the largest footprint is 20 (hyperreality). A citation is evidence of mention and not of need (D22), so these are loads in a model of the record and not claims about the repositories.

### 14.3 Deals: a vector, not a price `[FRAME]` (D24)

An *opening* takes a law *before* to a law *after* that permits more. It is valued by (`deals.opening_value`): the *freedom allowed*, the permissions it opens counted along each dimension of freedom (by default the action; a mapping can group actions); the *freedom enabled*, the states it makes reachable from where the parties are and the permissions the law grants there; and four yes-or-no *coherence* dimensions: **exists** (the system survives for ever from where it is), **corrigible** (and stays where an overseer can bring it home), **lawful** (nothing is permitted anywhere, reachable or not, that the greatest corrigible law forbids), **monotone** (nobody loses a permission they had). That is my reading of your three phrases, made finite (B13).

* *Closure.* A door opened onto a room that permits nothing leaves the system stuck in the doorway; the smallest coherent opening that contains it is the door plus what is behind it. A coherent opening is closed under what it enables: a permission whose consequences are not already permitted must come with them (a single permission is coherent only when everything it leads to is already permitted). In the hamlets no unilateral opening is coherent and the joint one is a win for both.
* *No trade-off within lawful existence.* An opening is coherent only if its law lies inside the greatest corrigible law L and contains the one before; L itself is such an opening whenever the law before lies inside it and the parties start in the corrigible kernel; and every coordinate (permissions opened, states reached, permissions there) is monotone in the law. So L is at least as good as every coherent opening in every coordinate at once, which is R2 in the language of deals. Checked over every law of every sampled small game. There is nothing for a price to settle among coherent openings: given non-rival permissions (B10) this kind of freedom is not scarce. What remains scarce in the model is certification, which is knowledge (§14.1), and understanding (§14.2).
* *Comprehension certifies.* A coalition that understands the material of an opening (T and everything T relies on, which is closed under the moves) can certify it exactly: its permission is in the coalition's certified law iff it is in the greatest law (§14.1, exactness). So an opening is admitted (`deals.admit`) only if it is coherent and the people deciding understand its footprint, and the two conditions meet.
* *A price is blind to the balanced deal.* A price is a strictly positive linear weighting. By the theorem of the alternative (Motzkin's transposition theorem, recalled) every option either has a price under which it ranks at least as high as every other (a tie counts: with options (1,1), (2,0) and (0,2) the first ranks level with the others at the price (1,1)) or is strictly dominated by a blend of the others, never both: `deals.supporting_weights` and `deals.dominating_combination` find the certificate exactly (vertex enumeration of a small LP, rational arithmetic), and a reader checks either without trusting the solver. An efficient option that is strictly beaten by a blend is chosen by no price. Worked: a hub with three doors, one onto ten rooms of x-freedoms, one onto ten of y-freedoms, one onto four rooms with both. Their values are (x 11), (y 11) and (x 5, y 4), with 10, 10 and 4 new states; all three are coherent and none dominates another; the third is below the half-and-half blend of the first two, and no x-price against any y-price, with any price on enabling and on coherence as well, chooses it.
* *Competition is the exception.* Among 236 random menus of what coalitions of one or two people can certify (500 random games in the test), 3 offered more than one efficient option and none hid one from every price; the ledger's smaller sample (31 menus) found none either (`test_in_random_games_a_menu_of_competing_options_is_the_exception_and_a_hidden_option_rarer_still`). A hidden option needs at least three efficient ones. The claim is about where scarcity enters, not how often.
* *Coherence is not a currency.* Put lawfulness in the price at any finite weight p and a game exists in which the lawless opening outscores the lawful one: a trap of (3p − 1)//7 + 1 rooms (integer division), because freedoms inside it can be made as numerous as you like and a finite price cannot outweigh them. Coherence is a constraint, admitted or not, never a weight.

### 14.4 Entangled decisions `[FRAME]` for the reading, `[FORM]` for the numbers (D27), `[HYPER]` for institutions (D28)

Read an *entangled decision* as parties who each hold part of a shared entangled state, each choose a measurement by an input only they know, and give an output, with no communication. Read an *exclusively win-win deal* as a *common-interest game*: everyone wins or loses together. The *classical value* is the best win probability of any strategy that uses only the party's own input and shared randomness; shared randomness mixes deterministic strategies and the value is linear in the mix, so the maximum is at a deterministic one, found by enumerating them all. The *quantum value* is the win probability of explicit local measurements on a shared state, computed exactly (`entangled.table`: the probability of every output for every input).

| game | parties | classical value | with the entangled state |
|---|---|---|---|
| GHZ (parity of three bits equals the OR of three inputs of even parity) | 3 | 3/4 | 1, with the GHZ state, measuring X on input 0 and Y on input 1 |
| Mermin–Peres magic square (agree on the shared cell of a 3 × 3 table, Alice's row of even parity and Bob's column of odd parity) | 2 | 8/9 | 1, with two entangled pairs; no classical ledger of nine signs satisfies every row and column |
| CHSH | 2 | 3/4 | 577/676 ≈ 0.85355 from rational angles on a Bell pair; the Tsirelson value (2 + √2)/4 ≈ 0.85355 is irrational, recalled, and reached to within about 3 × 10⁻⁶ |

*The value of the state.* For the state α|000⟩ + β|111⟩ and the GHZ measurements the win probability is exactly 1/2 + αβ/(α² + β²) = 1/2 + (sin 2t)/2 for α = cos t, β = sin t (checked on ten states): a product state is worth a coin toss with these measurements, and the state beats the classical 3/4 only beyond sin 2t = 1/2. So *"provides value by simply existing in an entangled state"* has one exact reading: for this family of states and these measurements the win probability is a formula in the degree of entanglement, and the advantage over the best classical strategy is positive only beyond a threshold; it is not a general theory of the value of entanglement. What holds in general is that a product state never beats the classical value with any measurements (it is a classical strategy).

*What it does not do.* (1) *It carries no message*: in every strategy here each party's output distribution depends only on its own input (no-signalling), so entanglement cannot replace disclosure; it helps where the parties cannot or will not communicate. A single bit of communication wins CHSH outright. (2) *It is consumed*: after the measurements the state is a product state, and a second round on it is worth no more than a classical one; every use needs a fresh resource, which costs. (3) *No-signalling is not what entanglement achieves*: a PR box is no-signalling and wins CHSH with certainty, which no quantum strategy does, so local strategies, quantum strategies and no-signalling tables are three different sets. (4) *Opposed interests are not modelled*: the claim that entanglement dissolves the prisoner's dilemma in quantum games depends on the strategy set allowed (Benjamin and Hayden; van Enk and Pike, recalled and not checked here), and a deal with opposed interests is not exclusively win-win. (5) *That institutional decisions can be entangled is a proposal* (D28): it would need separated devices, a Bell-type test with the locality loophole closed, and a stated cost per use for making and distributing the pairs. Nothing here has been run on a physical system.

### 14.5 Rival freedoms: an exchange of indivisible goods without money `[FRAME]` for the reading, `[FORM]` for the checks (D31)

Everything above gives away things that cost the giver nothing. A *rival* freedom is held by one party at a time (a slot, a seat, a unit that runs out): granting it to one party takes it from another, so a deal can leave someone worse off, and "exclusively win-win" (B10) needs a rule. This section is an extension I made, not something asked for in your follow-up; it answers the question B10 left open, and it is `[FRAME]`: a rival freedom is read as an indivisible good. The reading is Shapley and Scarf's *housing market*: n parties and n *indivisible* goods; the *endowment* gives each party one; each party has a *strict* ranking of all the goods (no ties) that only it knows; an *allocation* is any rearrangement of the goods among the parties. The rule is Gale's *top trading cycles* (TTC): every remaining party points at the owner of the good it ranks highest among those left; the pointers form at least one cycle (a party that already holds its favourite points at itself); the parties on each cycle trade along it and leave; repeat. No money changes hands and nobody sees another's ranking.

Four properties are what a win-win rule needs. Each is checked from its definition in `rival.py`, not assumed from the theorem it is known by:

* *individually rational*: no party ends with a good it ranks below the one it started with, so nobody loses by joining;
* *Pareto efficient*: no other allocation leaves every party no worse off and some party better off;
* *in the strict core*: no *blocking coalition* exists, that is no set of parties who, trading only the goods they started with among themselves, could leave each of them no worse off than in the outcome and one of them better off; and the outcome is the *only* allocation in the strict core (Roth and Postlewaite 1977, recalled; checked by listing the core of every profile);
* *strategy-proof*: no party gains, by its own ranking, from reporting a different ranking while the others tell the truth (Roth 1982, recalled), so private rankings can be asked for without a price. Misreports by groups of parties are not checked.

They hold on all 4 profiles of two parties and all 216 of three (the identity endowment stands for every endowment up to renaming the goods), on 151 seeded markets of four to six parties in the tests and 35 in the ledger, and on all 331,776 profiles of four by a long run outside the suite (`scripts/check_rival_exhaustive.py`, 2 minutes 40 seconds on four cores on 2026-10-11: every profile met every definition, and trade happened in 255,120 of them and in none of the other 76,656; these two counts are not recomputed by the suite). The Boston mechanism, a common rule for assigning goods, is the negative control: the same misreport checker finds a profitable lie in 36 of the 216 profiles of three parties, so the check can fail. An independent reference, written from a specification by a subagent, agrees on 9,020 comparisons in the full run (3,000 outcomes, three checkers on 5,600 allocations, 300 cores and 120 sets of misreports), and a planted slip on either side is caught (HO-024).

* *A swap a price cannot see.* A ranking can come from a party's private weights on the coordinates of a good's vector value (§14.3): `rival.rank_by_weights`. Take two slots, (5, 1) and (1, 5) in freedom of x and of y. A holds the y-rich one and weighs x three times as much as y; B holds the x-rich one and weighs y three times as much. Each doubles its worth by swapping (8 to 16). Under any common price the goods held are worth the same before and after any exchange of them, since a swap only permutes them, so no price registers the swap as a gain: a party that values each good at the common price is indifferent, or demands money. TTC makes the swap, with no money, from the two private rankings alone.
* *Trade happens exactly when the start is inefficient.* In 118 of the 216 profiles of three parties someone moves, and these are exactly the profiles in which the endowment is not Pareto efficient; in the other 98 everybody keeps what they hold. Nobody ends below where they began, and where anyone moves someone ends strictly higher.
* *Each party can audit the outcome.* The cycles are published round by round. A party checks, from its own ranking and that record alone, that in the round it traded it received its favourite among the goods still on the market (those held by parties who trade in that round or later). If every party's audit passes, the record describes the TTC outcome and no other: the first round's cycles are cycles of the first round's pointers, and the argument repeats on the market that is left. Checked on every record of three parties on 36 markets (864 records, 52 of them accepted by every party, all describing the TTC outcome) and of up to four parties on 60 random markets. The converse holds only for the honest record: the same final allocation reached in another order of rounds can fail an audit, because a party is then given less than its favourite on the market at that time. The audit catches a record that wrongs the party who audits; a party cannot tell from its own audit that another was wronged.
* *The start decides the rest.* With identical rankings nothing moves under any starting allocation (the holder of the best good keeps it), so each party's final place is its starting place, and over all starting allocations a party can end anywhere from its first choice to its last. Removing money removes the place where the starting allocation used to be settled. It does not remove the question: who holds what at the start is given here, not derived (B16).

Outside the model, so that the guarantees fail or change there: ties in the rankings, parties who want several goods, tastes that depend on who else gets what, groups who misreport together, parties who arrive over time, and a publisher of the record who might lie about it. The checks are exhaustive up to four parties and sampled beyond; they are not a proof for larger markets, and the theorems they meet are recalled, not proved here.

### 14.6 Conceptual blocks added by this layer

* **B10: win-win holds by construction here (D30).** Permissions are not rival, so granting one leaves every other holder's as it was; and in a common-interest game everyone wins together. "Win" means a permission nobody could certify before. This is a definition, not a discovery. With rival freedoms (a permission to use a unit of something that runs out) win-win fails for some deals, and an exchange without money is needed: top trading cycles (strategy-proof, Pareto-efficient and individually rational for strict preferences over indivisible goods; Shapley and Scarf, Roth) is the standard answer. It is built and checked from the definitions for indivisible goods held one to a party under strict rankings (§14.5, D31), where win-win holds in the weak sense that nobody ends below where they began; beyond that it is not.
* **B11: understanding has no instrument here (D29).** The barrier is only as honest as the test, and whoever certifies understanding holds the gate. The tension is quantified (the price of the barrier above) and not resolved. A prediction test against a held-back model is proposed.
* **B12: nothing competes within lawful existence.** Given non-rival permissions (B10) freedom of this kind is not scarce, so the role money plays in rationing moves: what remains scarce in the model is certification (knowledge) and understanding (the bus factor). That is why the deal is an exchange of certificates and the gate is understanding. Attention is not modelled, and whether scarcity of the first kind (rival goods, energy, time) can be handled the same way is open (B10).
* **B13: the coordinates of a deal are model-relative.** The dimension of freedom defaults to the action; the enabled freedom depends on where the parties start; the four coherence dimensions are the ones this model distinguishes. Another model would count differently, and the vector's order with it.
* **B14: pessimism is sound and not complete.** A view that is not closed under the moves it can make can fail to certify a permission that is in fact lawful (it treats the unknown as unsafe). That is the price of a certificate that never lies.
* **B15: the quantum layer is exact and local.** It shows what a shared state buys parties who cannot talk, in games small enough to enumerate. It does not show that the advantage survives noise, that it can be bought at a price an institution could pay, or that decisions are the kind of thing that can be entangled.
* **B16: the starting allocation is the price that remained (D31).** Taking money out of an exchange of rival goods leaves one thing that money used to settle: who holds what at the start. Top trading cycles is individually rational relative to that start, so it cannot be fairer than the start; with identical rankings nothing moves at all, and each party's final place is its starting place. This package derives a greatest sustaining law (§3) and no starting allocation: nothing here says who should hold which rival freedom. It is an input, named, and open (`nd-endowment`, HO-027).
